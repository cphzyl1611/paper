\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

Prior work supplies several components needed for APT defense evaluation, but these components use different evidence and units of analysis. Attack knowledge bases describe adversary behavior; detection systems transform telemetry into alerts or attack reconstructions; network-enforcement systems implement and evaluate specific controls; and vulnerability or attack-graph metrics estimate structural risk. We organize the literature around these roles and distinguish their outputs from the action--outcome--control tuple scored in this paper.

\subsection{Attack Knowledge Bases and Adversary-Emulation Evaluation}
\label{subsec:rw-knowledge-emulation}

MITRE ATT\&CK provides the common tactic and technique vocabulary used to normalize adversary behavior in our benchmark~\cite{mitreAttack}. ATT\&CK Evaluations add a different kind of evidence: they record how products expose or detect individual procedures during adversary emulation~\cite{mitreAttackEvaluations}. These step-level observations are a natural input to a scoring framework, but they remain observations until they are aligned with benchmark actions and assigned a consistent outcome.

D3FEND organizes cybersecurity countermeasures and their relationships to adversary behaviors~\cite{mitreD3FEND,kaloroumakis2021d3fend}. Its role is therefore prospective: given an adversary behavior, it identifies defensive techniques that may be relevant. Our attribution layer addresses the reverse, evidence-driven question. Given an action and an observed defense result, it identifies the victim-side control point that failed first and is most relevant for repair. D3FEND can inform the defense-technology crosswalk, but a capability relationship alone does not establish that a deployed system prevented, blocked, detected, or missed an action.

Academic work has also examined how attack knowledge is extracted and represented. iACE extracts indicators and contextual relations from open-source threat reports~\cite{liao2016ioc}, while ATTACK2VEC learns temporal relationships among attack steps observed in large-scale security events~\cite{shen2019attack2vec}. Li et al. show that operational threat-intelligence feeds differ substantially in coverage and timeliness~\cite{li2019tealeaves}, motivating explicit provenance for benchmark mappings rather than treating all threat descriptions as equivalent evidence.

A separate line connects high-level attack descriptions to system evidence. POIROT converts threat-intelligence descriptions into query graphs and aligns them with kernel-audit provenance through inexact graph matching~\cite{milajerdi2019poirot}. ATLAS instead learns sequences of attack symptoms and constructs attack stories for investigation~\cite{alsaheel2021atlas}. These systems narrow the semantic gap between an attack description and low-level observations. Their output, however, is matched activity or an attack reconstruction; they do not define how heterogeneous observations should be normalized into defense outcomes or aggregated into repair-oriented scores.

\subsection{APT Detection, Correlation, and Attack Investigation}
\label{subsec:rw-detection-investigation}

Early provenance systems relied on explicit causal or behavioral structure. SLEUTH propagates tags through commodity audit events to reconstruct causal attack scenarios in real time~\cite{hossain2017sleuth}. HOLMES correlates suspicious information flows into higher-level APT scenarios using knowledge of adversarial behaviors~\cite{milajerdi2019holmes}. Both preserve multi-step context, but their primary result is an attack graph or campaign-level detection rather than a measure of how well a defense stack handled each benchmark action.

Other systems focus on reducing investigation cost or improving evidence fidelity. NoDoze combines provenance context with anomaly scoring to prioritize alerts and reduce the graphs presented to analysts~\cite{hassan2019nodoze}. OmegaLog correlates application and system logs so that investigators can recover higher-fidelity attack provenance without relying on either log layer alone~\cite{hassan2020omegalog}. Their contribution is not simply ``better detection'': NoDoze addresses triage, whereas OmegaLog addresses cross-layer evidence reconstruction.

Learning-based approaches use different representations and output granularities. DeepLog models ordered log events with recurrent neural networks and reports anomalous execution sequences~\cite{du2017deeplog}. Log2Vec constructs heterogeneous enterprise-log graphs and learns embeddings for threat detection~\cite{liu2019log2vec}. These systems operate on log sequences or entities, whereas the scoring unit in this paper is a benchmark action aligned with a defense observation.

Within provenance-based detection, UNICORN summarizes long-running system provenance and learns unsupervised models for low-and-slow APT behavior~\cite{han2020unicorn}. Tactical provenance analysis raises endpoint events to behavior-level abstractions useful to EDR investigation~\cite{hassan2020tactical}, while threaTrace learns node-level representations to detect and trace suspicious host entities~\cite{wang2022threatrace}. These differences matter because an anomaly, a tactic-level abstraction, and a suspicious node are not interchangeable observations; each requires a separate alignment rule before it can support action-level scoring.

Recent work has concentrated on deployment scale, online processing, and interpretable output. DISTDET distributes detection across hosts and performs higher-level correlation for large enterprise deployments~\cite{dong2023distdet}. PROGRAPHER limits provenance dependency expansion by embedding time-ordered graph snapshots~\cite{yang2023prographer}. NODLINK formulates online provenance-graph construction as an online Steiner Tree Problem and uses an approximation algorithm to recover concise, fine-grained attack graphs~\cite{li2024nodlink}. TAPAS instead segments process provenance by task to reduce the spatial and temporal cost of long-running online detection~\cite{zhang2025tapas}. The four systems therefore address different deployment constraints: distributed correlation, dependency control, concise online investigation, and long-duration graph segmentation.

Representation-learning systems make another set of tradeoffs. MAGIC uses masked graph representation learning for entity- and batch-level detection and includes adaptation for concept drift~\cite{jia2024magic}. KAIROS learns the temporal evolution of whole-system provenance, assigns fine-grained event anomaly scores, and reconstructs compact attack summaries~\cite{cheng2024kairos}. FLASH also learns provenance representations for scalable intrusion detection, but uses a different semantic and structural representation design~\cite{rehman2024flash}. R-CAID is particularly relevant to our attribution discussion because it incorporates root-cause analysis into provenance-based detection~\cite{goyal2024rcaid}. Its output identifies the causal origin of suspicious activity in the observed provenance. Our first-failed control point instead identifies the defensive mechanism or policy that should be repaired. The two forms of explanation are complementary but not equivalent.

Collectively, detection and investigation systems can supply alerts, suspicious entities, compact subgraphs, attack stories, or causal roots. Our framework treats these artifacts as possible defense observations. It adds an evaluation layer that aligns them with benchmark actions, normalizes them into \texttt{PREVENTED}, \texttt{BLOCKED}, \texttt{DETECTED}, or \texttt{MISSED}, and retains a link from score loss to a repairable victim-side control point.

\subsection{Network Enforcement and Defense-System Evaluation}
\label{subsec:rw-network-enforcement}

Network-enforcement research provides examples of controls whose effects can be observed directly during replay. SANE centralizes enterprise connectivity decisions in a protected network architecture~\cite{casado2006sane}; its contribution is architectural prevention rather than post hoc detection. FIREMAN analyzes firewall rules and topology to identify conflicts and policy misconfigurations before deployment~\cite{yuan2006fireman}. These works illustrate two different evidence types: an enforced communication decision and a static finding about policy correctness.

Software-defined networking introduced mechanisms for both active defense and systematic assessment. AVANT-GUARD moves connection management and event triggers toward OpenFlow switches to protect the controller and enable responsive enforcement~\cite{shin2013avantguard}. SPHINX monitors evolving SDN state and detects behavior inconsistent with the expected network model~\cite{dhawan2015sphinx}. DELTA takes an assessment-oriented approach, orchestrating known attacks and protocol-aware fuzzing against SDN controllers and applications~\cite{lee2017delta}. AVANT-GUARD and SPHINX are defenses that can emit blocking or detection evidence; DELTA is closer to the replay infrastructure that generates such evidence.

Programmable data planes support higher-rate and more specialized enforcement. Poseidon implements configurable volumetric-DDoS detection and mitigation in programmable switches~\cite{zhang2020poseidon}. Jaqen builds switch-native detection primitives and network-wide resource management for dynamic high-rate attacks~\cite{liu2021jaqen}. Ripple targets adaptive link-flooding through decentralized, declaratively programmed switch defenses~\cite{xing2021ripple}. SmartCookie combines programmable-switch SYN-cookie checks with an eBPF proxy to block SYN floods while preserving benign-service performance~\cite{yoo2024smartcookie}. Their evaluations are not directly interchangeable because the systems differ in attack model, enforcement location, platform requirements, and reported output.

Such systems provide the kind of empirical evidence required by our scoring layer: a flow may be impossible by design, dropped during execution, observed without interruption, or missed. Conventional evaluations report mechanism-specific quantities such as detection accuracy, blocking rate, throughput, latency, and overhead. Our framework does not replace those measurements. It aligns them with playbook actions and aggregates the resulting outcomes across stages and attack paths.

The full attribution schema in this paper is broader than network enforcement: it also covers endpoint, identity, data, and operational controls. A Mininet-based experiment therefore constitutes a network-replayable validation slice rather than experimental coverage of the complete taxonomy. Actions that require unsupported endpoint state must be reported as out of scope or explicit experimental preconditions, not as defense failures.

\subsection{Vulnerability, Attack-Graph, and Defense-Effectiveness Metrics}
\label{subsec:rw-metrics}

Vulnerability scores and attack graphs quantify security using evidence that differs from runtime defense observations. CVSS estimates the severity of an individual vulnerability from exploitability, impact, and environmental factors~\cite{mell2007common,first2019cvss31}. Its unit is the vulnerability, not an adversary action together with a product response and a failed control.

Attack-graph methods reason over configurations, privileges, and reachability. Sheyner et al. generate attack graphs through symbolic model checking~\cite{sheyner2002attackgraphs}. MulVAL derives attack paths from logical facts about hosts, vulnerabilities, and connectivity~\cite{ou2005mulval}, and later work improves attack-graph generation at larger network scale~\cite{ou2006scalable}. These methods preserve path dependencies that simple vulnerability counts miss, but the paths describe what may be reachable under a model rather than what a deployed defense actually observed during replay.

Security metrics extend this structural view. The $k$-zero-day safety metric measures how many unknown vulnerabilities an attacker would require to compromise a target~\cite{wang2014kzero}. Network-diversity metrics estimate resilience arising from heterogeneous software and configurations~\cite{zhang2016networkdiversity}. Both are useful structural measures, but neither should be reported as empirical detection or blocking performance. More broadly, Herley and van Oorschot argue that security claims require explicit assumptions, evidence, and limits of inference~\cite{herley2017science}. Following this principle, our reporting separates \emph{structural applicability}, derived from the defense crosswalk, from \emph{empirical defense performance}, derived from observed replay traces.

\subsection{Synthesis and Positioning}
\label{subsec:rw-position}

Table~\ref{tab:related-work-position} summarizes the distinction across research lines. The proposed framework does not claim to replace attack vocabularies, provenance detectors, network controls, or attack-graph metrics. Simply combining their outputs is insufficient because they use incompatible evidence semantics and units of analysis: an alert, a suspicious subgraph, a blocking decision, and a prospective path-risk value do not identify the same evaluated event and cannot be aggregated without an explicit alignment rule. Our framework supplies that missing layer by mapping heterogeneous observations to benchmark actions, normalizing their outcomes, preserving stage logic, and attributing score loss to a first-failed victim-side control point. The framework and full benchmark remain broader than the network testbed; the planned Mininet evaluation is an empirical validation slice over network-replayable actions rather than experimental coverage of the complete taxonomy. This action--outcome--control unit permits stage-aware aggregation while keeping an aggregate loss traceable to a concrete remediation target.
